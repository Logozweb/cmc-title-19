Can I Own a Home and Qualify for Medicaid in Connecticut?

By Akua Achiaa Okyere, MBA (Former CT DSS Professional)
Last Regulatory Review: September 2026
6-Minute Read

Yes — in many situations, owning a home does not automatically prevent you from qualifying for Connecticut Medicaid (Title 19 / HUSKY C).

However, the complete answer depends directly on which Medicaid coverage group you are applying for, whether you are seeking institutional Long-Term Services & Supports (LTSS), your equity interest in the property, who currently lives in the residence, and what actions were taken with the property during the 60-month look-back period.

That distinction is critical. Families frequently hear “you can keep your house” and falsely assume real estate has zero bearing on Medicaid. Conversely, others hear “Medicaid will immediately seize your house” and panic into rushed, catastrophic deed transfers. Neither statement is accurate. Here is what Connecticut families must understand under state regulations.

1. Owning a Primary Residence Is Not an Automatic Disqualification

For Connecticut Medicaid eligibility purposes, an applicant’s principal residence is treated as an exempt or non-countable asset while the applicant (or their protected spouse) lives there. Unlike liquid cash, checking accounts, CDs, or stocks—which are capped at the strict $1,600 individual asset limit—a primary home does not count toward that ceiling.

This means you should never assume that home ownership alone bars you from seeking Medicaid long-term care assistance.

2026 Connecticut Statutory Standard
Under Connecticut Department of Social Services (DSS) standards, the excess home equity limit for an individual applicant receiving institutional LTSS is $1,130,000. If home equity exceeds this threshold, institutional Medicaid eligibility is restricted unless specific statutory exemptions apply.

2. Home Equity Limitations for Medicaid LTSS

Equity is calculated as the fair market value of the applicant’s ownership interest minus any mortgages, lines of credit (HELOCs), or recorded liens. If an unmarried senior moves permanently into a nursing home and holds over $1,130,000 in equity, Connecticut DSS will require a spend-down or equity reduction before approving LTSS benefits.

Crucially, Connecticut law waives this equity cap entirely if any of the following individuals lawfully reside in the home:

  • The applicant’s community spouse
  • A minor child under age 21
  • An adult child who is blind or permanently and totally disabled under Social Security standards

3. What If My Spouse Continues Living in the Home?

Married couples receive the strongest statutory protections under Connecticut's spousal impoverishment provisions. When one spouse requires skilled nursing facility care and the other spouse remains at home, the residence remains 100% exempt regardless of market value.

The community spouse cannot be forced to sell the home, take out a reverse mortgage, or vacate the premises. Furthermore, Connecticut allows the institutionalized spouse to transfer their entire ownership interest to the community spouse with zero transfer penalty.

Critical Warning on Quitclaim Deeds & Gifting
Never sign a quitclaim deed transferring your home to adult children for $1.00 before consulting a professional. CT DSS audits all property transfers within 60 months. Gifting a home triggers an immediate, multi-year Medicaid penalty period where DSS pays $0 toward nursing home bills ($16,000+/month).

4. Permitted Exceptions to Home Transfer Penalties

While gifting a home generally causes severe transfer penalties, Connecticut law does recognize narrow exceptions where a home can be transferred penalty-free:

  • Spousal Transfer: Transferring sole title to a community spouse.
  • Caretaker Child Exception: Transferring title to an adult son or daughter who lived in the home for at least 2 full years immediately prior to nursing home admission, and whose verified caregiving prevented earlier institutionalization.
  • Sibling With Equity Interest: Transferring to a sibling who already holds an equity ownership stake in the home and resided there for at least 1 full year prior to admission.
  • Disabled Child: Transferring to a child of any age who is blind or permanently disabled.

5. Eligibility vs. Post-Death Estate Recovery

One of the most dangerous points of confusion for Connecticut families is conflating initial Medicaid eligibility with post-death estate recovery.

While DSS cannot force you to sell your home while alive to qualify, Connecticut operates an active Medicaid Estate Recovery Program. Upon the death of a Medicaid recipient (and following the death of a surviving spouse), DSS may file a claim against the probate estate to recover the cost of long-term care benefits paid.

Structuring deeds, trusts, life estates, or survivorship interests to protect against estate recovery requires specialized legal drafting. 6. Pre-Application Property Checklist

Before filing a Title 19 application or making any property changes, gather the following records:

  • A copy of the current recorded deed (Warranty, Quitclaim, or Survivorship Deed)
  • Recent municipal property tax assessments showing assessed market value
  • Current mortgage, home-equity loan, or lien balance statements
  • Homeowners insurance policy declaration pages
  • Utility bills verifying primary residence occupancy

The Bottom Line

You do not have to forfeit your family home to receive Medicaid in Connecticut. But every step—from documenting intent to return, managing home equity, and shielding the community spouse—must follow precise Department of Social Services protocols.
