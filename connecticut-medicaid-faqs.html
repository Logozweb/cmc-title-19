Medicaid, Title 19 & HUSKY C

Questions and procedural guidance for Connecticut families and providers.

Title XIX of the Social Security Act is the federal foundation for the Medicaid program. In Connecticut, residents, healthcare providers, and social workers commonly use the term 'Title 19' interchangeably with Medicaid, specifically when discussing long-term institutional care and Home and Community-Based Services (HCBS).

Yes. Title XIX establishes federal Medicaid authorization, while Connecticut administers the benefit locally under the umbrella of HUSKY Health. When people in Connecticut say they need 'Title 19 assistance,' they are referring to Medicaid coverage administered by the Connecticut Department of Social Services (DSS).

HUSKY C is Connecticut's dedicated Medicaid category for individuals aged 65 and older, individuals who are blind, and individuals with certified qualifying disabilities. It covers comprehensive healthcare services and includes Long-Term Services and Supports (LTSS) for institutional nursing home care or home-based waiver care.

No. While HUSKY C covers skilled nursing home residency, it also funds Long-Term Services & Supports for seniors living at home or in certified residential settings through Connecticut Home Care Program for Elders (CHCPE) waivers and personal care assistance programs.

Compass specializes specifically in HUSKY C, institutional Long-Term Services & Supports (LTSS), nursing home Medicaid, and Home & Community-Based Services (HCBS). We do not handle standard HUSKY A (family) or HUSKY D (expanded Medicaid for low-income non-disabled adults under 65) applications.

Yes. Individuals enrolled in both are known as 'dual-eligible.' Medicare serves as primary payer for acute care, physician visits, and brief rehabilitation, while Connecticut Medicaid (HUSKY C) acts as secondary payer and covers custodial nursing care and long-term personal assistance that Medicare excludes entirely.

Federal Spousal Impoverishment provisions protect the healthy spouse (known as the 'Community Spouse'). No. Deeding real estate or transferring money without fair market compensation during the 60-month lookback period triggers severe transfer penalty periods during which Medicaid refuses to pay nursing home costs. Families should never transfer deeds without qualified elder law advice.

When assets are gifted or transferred for less than fair market value within 60 months, DSS divides the total uncompensated value by the State's average monthly private nursing home rate. The result represents the exact number of months Medicaid will deny benefits, starting when the applicant is otherwise eligible.

Once approved for institutional Medicaid, the resident cannot keep their entire monthly pension or Social Security. Instead, after deducting a $75 personal needs allowance and permitted spousal income diversions, the remaining monthly income must be paid directly to the nursing home as 'applied income.'

If an individual in the community has income exceeding the strict HUSKY C medically needy income limit, DSS establishes a spend-down budget period (usually 6 months). When the applicant incurs qualifying medical bills equal to the excess income difference, Medicaid activates for the remainder of the period.

No. A 'medical spend-down' refers strictly to offsetting excess monthly income against incurred healthcare bills. Permitted asset spend-down, on the other hand, allows families to pay off legitimate debts, purchase irrevocable burial trusts, or make necessary home modifications without gifting penalties.

Because asset ceilings ($1,600 for single individuals), spousal CSRA caps, MMMNA standards, and home equity limits are updated annually by DSS and federal CMS, families should always rely on current official DSS publications or professional consultation rather than outdated online articles.

Annual Renewals & Redeterminations

Questions and procedural guidance for Connecticut families and providers.

No. Federal and state law requires annual redetermination of eligibility. Connecticut DSS conducts annual reviews to verify that the beneficiary remains below countable asset limits ($1,600) and that all income sources and living arrangements continue to meet program requirements.

Renewals are processed using Form W-1ER (Renewal Form) received by mail, or digitally via the online ConneCT / MyDSS client portal. Beneficiaries must submit updated financial statements, proof of current income, and verification of ongoing medical expenses.

Submitting the renewal form is only the first step. If bank balances have fluctuated or new income is recorded, DSS issues a supplemental verification notice. Failure to submit requested proof by the specified deadline results in immediate benefits closure.

Yes. Our Annual Redetermination Shield service assists families regardless of who prepared the initial application. We audit bank balances, compile mandatory verifications, verify allowable spend-downs, and submit complete renewal binders to prevent coverage gaps.

Connecticut DSS provides a 90-day Reconsideration Period following procedural closure. If the complete renewal packet and verifications are submitted within this 90-day window, DSS can reinstate benefits retroactively to the date of closure without requiring a new baseline application.
